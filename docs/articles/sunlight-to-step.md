# From Sunlight to the Finish Line.

<!-- section: opening -->
## Photon.

The Sun is mostly hydrogen.

Its enormous mass gives it a strong gravitational pull.
As it formed, gravity pulled the gas inward.
The gas compressed.
Pressure increased.
Temperature rose.

Deep in the core, it became hot and dense enough for nuclear fusion to begin.

Hydrogen nuclei—the centres of hydrogen atoms—combine through a chain of reactions.
The net result is four hydrogen nuclei becoming one helium nucleus.
Other particles are released.
So is energy.

And the process keeps repeating.

This is what powers the Sun.

<!-- note: quantum -->

But where does that energy come from?

After the reactions, the final particles have slightly less mass than the starting material.
That difference appears as energy.

Einstein connected the dots.

**E = mc².**

Take the difference in mass.
Multiply it by the speed of light squared.

The mass difference is tiny.
The multiplier is enormous.

It is a lot of energy.

But how much?

Imagine starting with one gram of hydrogen.
Let it complete the Sun’s fusion process.

Most of the mass remains in the helium.
Only about **0.7%** becomes released energy.

Think of a car with a 50-litre fuel tank.
Now picture **400 of those tanks**, all full of gasoline.
Together, that fuel contains roughly the same amount of energy.

And we started with just one gram of hydrogen.

<!-- note: mass -->

## Light.

Some of the energy released in these reactions is carried by photons.

But we are still deep inside the Sun.
The surface is hundreds of thousands of kilometres away.
And there is no clear path out.

Radiation keeps interacting with the surrounding material.
Photons are absorbed.
New ones are emitted.
Others are scattered.

A short step.
Another interaction.
A new direction.

Sometimes outward.
Sometimes back towards the centre.

Energy passes between radiation and matter.
Gradually, it moves outward.

How long does this take?

One solar model puts this slow, random journey at about **170,000 years**.
With roughly **ten trillion trillion interactions** along the way.

And that only gets us through the dense inner layers.
We are not at the surface yet.

Farther out, hot material rises.
It carries energy towards the surface.
This part is much quicker.

So we are following the energy.
Not one original photon travelling all the way from the core.

Eventually, light escapes into space.

Now, what exactly is leaving the Sun?

Light carries energy.
When it is emitted or absorbed, that energy is exchanged in individual packets.

We call each packet a **photon**.

It is not an atom.
It is not an electron.
And it is not a tiny piece of hydrogen thrown out of the Sun.

Light has both wave-like and particle-like properties.

I still find that hard to visualise.

I can picture a wave.
I can picture a small ball.
But neither gives me the full picture of a photon.

So, for now, I will follow what it does.

It carries energy.
When it is absorbed, its journey ends.
Its energy is transferred.

<!-- note: photons -->

Our story follows that energy.
For this part of the journey, it travels as light.

So let’s follow a photon.

<!-- section: leaf -->

Sunlight spreads out into space.

A photon heading towards Earth now has a much clearer path.
The journey takes a little over eight minutes.

Only a small fraction of the Sun’s light reaches our planet.

Some is reflected back into space.
Some is absorbed by the atmosphere.
Some passes through and reaches the ground.

We follow one that gets through.

It reaches a field of wheat.

Then a leaf.

## Quantum physics

### From an atom to a plasma.

A neutral hydrogen-1 atom has one positively charged proton and one negatively charged electron. Their electrical attraction binds the electron to the nucleus.

In the Sun’s core, energetic collisions prevent electrons from remaining bound to individual nuclei. Nuclei and free electrons form a hot mixture called plasma.

### Why do the nuclei resist joining?

Ordinary hydrogen nuclei are single protons. Their positive charges repel. Heat makes them move rapidly. High density brings frequent encounters. But neither removes the electrical repulsion.

### Quantum tunnelling.

Quantum physics describes particles using probability waves. There is a small chance of finding two approaching protons close enough to interact even when they lack the energy to overcome their repulsion classically. This is quantum tunnelling.

There is no physical tunnel. No extra energy appears. We can calculate the probability, but not predict each individual outcome with certainty.

### Getting close does not guarantee fusion.

In the first proton–proton reaction, one proton changes into a neutron through the weak nuclear interaction. A neutron is a nuclear particle with no electric charge. The proton and neutron bind to form deuterium, a heavier form of hydrogen.

The reaction also releases a positron—the electron’s positively charged counterpart—and a neutrino, a particle that interacts very weakly with matter.

### Across the whole chain.

For the main proton–proton branch, a compact net description is:
4 hydrogen nuclei → 1 helium-4 nucleus + 2 positrons + 2 electron neutrinos + released energy.

This is the result of several reactions, not one four-way collision. The helium-4 nucleus has two protons and two neutrons. The positrons then annihilate with electrons, releasing additional radiation. The atomic-mass comparison in the next note includes this annihilation energy.

## Mass & energy

### What Einstein’s equation means.

E = mc² relates rest energy to rest mass. E is energy. m is mass. c is the speed of light in a vacuum: 299,792,458 metres per second. The ² means multiplying that speed by itself.

This relationship comes from special relativity. It tells us how a mass difference relates to released energy. It does not explain the reaction mechanism; that requires nuclear and quantum physics.

### Use the difference, not all the mass.

For a reaction, energy released = Δm × c². The symbol Δm means the decrease in the total rest mass of the reacting material.

The helium nucleus is a more tightly bound arrangement. The lower rest mass reflects lower stored energy—not missing protons or neutrons. Energy is conserved when we include the radiation and moving particles released.

### One gram, worked through.

Comparing the measured masses of four hydrogen-1 atoms and one helium-4 atom gives a decrease of about 0.7%. Using atomic masses accounts for electrons across the complete chain, including positron annihilation.

Using the rounded 0.7% for one gram of hydrogen:

Mass difference ≈ 0.007 grams = 0.000007 kilograms.

Energy ≈ 0.000007 × (299,792,458)².

Result ≈ 630 billion joules ≈ 175,000 kilowatt-hours.

A joule and a kilowatt-hour are units of energy. One kilowatt-hour equals 3.6 million joules. These figures are calculated and rounded, not measurements of a power plant.

### The fuel-tank comparison.

This is a calculated comparison of energy amounts. We assume a 50-litre tank; this is an example, not a claim about the average car.

The U.S. Department of Energy lists 112,114–116,090 Btu per U.S. gallon for gasoline/E10, using the lower heating value. This is about 31–32 megajoules per litre. A full 50-litre tank therefore contains about 1.6 billion joules of combustion energy.

Divide the approximate 630 billion joules released by fusing one gram of hydrogen by about 1.6 billion joules per tank. The result is roughly 400 full tanks.

The comparison is between total fusion energy and fuel energy. It is not usable electricity, engine output, or a prediction of driving distance. Some fusion energy escapes with neutrinos; converting energy into useful work also involves losses.

## Light & photons

### Where does a photon come from?

In one step of the Sun’s main fusion chain, a deuterium nucleus captures another proton. They form helium-3: two protons and one neutron. A gamma-ray photon carries away part of the released energy.

Gamma rays and visible light are both electromagnetic radiation. A gamma-ray photon carries much more energy than a visible-light photon.

### How long, and how many interactions?

Mitalas and Sills (1992) calculated about 170,000 years and 10²⁵ random-walk steps for radiation to diffuse from the centre to the base of the Sun’s convection zone. In this model, steps represent travel between interactions. 10²⁵ is ten trillion trillion: a one followed by 25 zeros.

These are model estimates, not a measured itinerary. The step length changes with the density and opacity of the solar material. The outer convective stage is much quicker.

### We are following energy, not one surviving photon.

Absorption destroys a photon and transfers its energy to matter. Emission creates a new photon. Scattering can change a photon’s direction and energy. Repeating these processes redistributes energy among many particles and photons. The narrative follows energy and its transformations, not a tagged packet that remains unchanged. Once light has escaped the Sun, we can focus on a photon travelling towards Earth. When that photon is absorbed, its journey ends; its energy has been transferred.

The random-walk calculation is a model of radiation transport. It does not mean one identifiable fusion photon survives to become the sunlight reaching a leaf. Most visible sunlight escapes from the photosphere, the layer we see as the Sun’s surface. It is not a solid boundary.

### Waves and particles.

Light spreads and interferes in wave-like ways. But it exchanges energy with matter in individual packets: photons. These are two aspects of the same phenomenon, not a tiny object switching between two forms.

A photon is not an atom or an electron. It has no rest mass, but it carries energy and momentum. Its energy depends on its frequency. Higher-frequency light carries more energy per photon.
